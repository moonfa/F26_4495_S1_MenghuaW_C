How this page works

  
The page answers two questions, not "is my view right":

  
  1. What does today's price imply? Take the company's reported or expected earnings (or cash flow), project them forward, multiply by an exit multiple, and read the annual return. If that return is below what you require, the price already asks you to believe more than the numbers show.
    2. 
  
  2. Which of several companies offers more? Run the same rules on each and compare annual returns on the same horizon.

  
The workflow

  
  1. Compare (first tab): enter 2–5 tickers. Every method with enough data runs from the company's own numbers. Read the table, then open one company.
    2. 
  
  2. Company: import the data, look at what the market pays (step 1 and the reference multiples), choose a method, reset to the status quo and change one input at a time. Save a snapshot when you want to keep it.

  
Reading a result

  
  • Annual price return = (scenario price ÷ today's price)1/years − 1. Dividends are not included.
    • 
  
  • Bear / Base / Bull are your ranges around the base case, not probabilities or limits. A Bear case is not a stop level.
    • 
  
  • Where the return comes from splits it exactly into earnings (or cash-flow) growth and multiple change. A return that depends on the multiple rising is a different bet from one that depends on growth.
    • 
  
  • What it would take gives the EPS, cash flow or exit multiple needed to reach your required return.
    • 
  
  • Checks point out inputs that look unusual (an exit multiple far above today's, a stale or one-off earnings base). They never change the numbers.

  
Choosing the exit multiple

  
The multiple is what the market pays for a growth and risk profile at the exit date, not today. A company growing 20% now may grow 10% in three years and be priced like it. The page therefore shows several anchors and does not pick one for you:

  
  • Today: what the market pays now on each starting number (trailing, this year's or next year's analyst EPS). The row matching your starting point is the default.
  • Own history: annual fiscal-year-end P/E, a rough guide to what this company has typically been valued at.
  • Peers: the median of the companies you enter, or of the largest companies in the stock's Yahoo industry when you tick the option; better evidence than a rule of thumb, but only as good as the set.
  • Sector rule of thumb: rough long-run bands kept in core/multiples.py; editable and not market data.
  • Growth-implied: peer median PEG (else 1–2) × the growth rate you expect near exit, a sanity check on whether the growth you assume supports the multiple.

  
Today's multiple or the historical one? Neither alone. Today's multiple prices today's growth; the historical one prices the growth and rates of another period. At exit the company will probably grow more slowly, so start from today's multiple as the neutral case, then look at where peers and history sit, and ask what growth the exit multiple needs. If the exit multiple is above every reference, the result mostly bets on the multiple.

  
In Compare, the "if the multiple de-rates" column repeats the calculation with the exit multiple lowered to the reference when today's is above it. A large gap means much of the return depends on the market continuing to pay today's multiple.

  
The methods

  
MethodFormulaBest forAvoid for

P/FCFFCF per share × ((1+growth)/(1+share change))t × exit P/FCFCapital-heavy or cash-generative companies (semiconductors, large tech, consumer)Negative or erratic free cash flow

Using more than one method on the same company is a cross-check, not an average. When methods disagree by 10 points or more, the answer depends on which earnings or cash-flow base you trust.

  
How Compare works, and what to use it for

  
For each ticker the page runs every method that fits and has data, from the company's own numbers: the starting point is the latest analyst estimate or trailing result, growth is the analyst forecast (fading to a long-run rate), and the exit multiple is today's multiple on that same starting number. Each result is an annual price return over your holding period. It does not say a stock is cheap or expensive. It shows how much return today's price leaves if expectations come true and the multiple does not change.

  
  • Use it to rank by how much has to go right, and to notice where the return depends on the multiple (the de-rating column) or on one method only (the gap flag).
    • 
  
  • ★ marks the method the fit rules prefer for that kind of company (for cyclicals, free cash flow rather than P/E). It is a rule-based preference, not a reliability rating, and a method with no flag is not risk-free. The ranking uses the most conservative applicable method, not the star.
    • 
  
  • Not run means the method does not suit the company or has no usable data. Not ranked means a default result looked implausible (above +60% or below −50% a year, usually a data mismatch such as an ADR share ratio or a very small base) or a share count did not reconcile: the numbers stay visible, but the company is left out of the ranking, because removing the odd method would make the remaining ones look better.
    • 
  
  • Companies with no analyst forecasts cannot be compared on growth; they show why.

  
Starting points, and the multiple that goes with them

  
TTM is what the company earned over the last four quarters. Analyst consensus (this year, next year) are expectations for fiscal years that end in the future. Reported annual figures are past fiscal years. A multiple always belongs to a number: 27.8× on trailing EPS and 12.1× on next year's analyst EPS are the same price read two ways. The page therefore pairs each starting point with today's multiple on that number (price ÷ it), applies your growth for the holding period from that number, and reads the exit multiple on the same basis. Holding the multiple flat then adds nothing from the multiple: the return is the growth you assume, and a change of basis cannot masquerade as a re-rating.

  
By default the page starts from the furthest analyst year, because that is the most recent forward-looking number there is, and grows it at the analyst rate fading to a long-run rate. Switch the starting point in the list to see how much the answer depends on the choice.

  
When earnings are far from what analysts expect

  
A company whose trailing EPS has collapsed (a trough, a write-down, a bad year) can trade at a high trailing P/E and a low forward one, because the market is pricing the recovery analysts forecast. Two readings go wrong here. Applying the high trailing multiple to the recovered earnings counts the recovery twice and gives an absurd return; ignoring the recovery altogether values the company on its worst year. The page shows both numbers side by side, starts from the analyst year, and keeps the multiple the market pays on it. Then the question that matters is a different one: what would you have to believe? The table under the result shows the annual return for a range of exit multiples (today's, own history, peers, sector) and for earnings that fall short or never recover. Whether the recovery will happen is the research question, and belongs in your thesis. Analyst EPS is usually an adjusted figure while reported EPS includes one-offs, so part of a gap can be accounting basis rather than turnaround.

  
Four layers, kept apart

  
  1. Reported history: company figures through the data provider, with period and fetch date. “Reported” is not “recurring”.
  2. Market consensus: analyst mean EPS and revenue; not a guarantee, and the adjusted-or-reported basis is unverified.
  3. Assumptions: your growth, fade, exit multiple, and the program's defaults for them. These are not forecasts of fact.
  4. Conditional results: scenario prices and annual returns. “Meets your requirement” only says the arithmetic clears the bar, not that the assumptions are likely.

  
Two methods that agree are not two independent opinions when they share the same growth input: P/FCF reuses analyst EPS or revenue growth because no cash-flow forecast exists.

  
What gets saved

  
  • Import latest data stores one data record (what the provider returned). Compare also stores data records for the tickers it loads, and reuses one younger than 6 hours.
  • Save snapshot stores your assumptions and the result as an analysis record. Typing and switching tabs only preview; nothing is written.
  • Nothing is overwritten. The Saved snapshots list shows every record, newest first, in your local time.
  • The workbench company page reads two things: the newest data record, and the newest analysis record of each method. Data records you did not choose a number from have no effect on it. Your Plan is never changed by any of this.

  
Free cash flow: before and after stock compensation

  
Free cash flow is operating cash flow minus capital expenditure. “After stock compensation” also subtracts the shares the company pays staff with, which is a real cost to owners that cash flow does not show. For companies where stock compensation is a large share of free cash flow (many technology firms) the after-compensation figure is the fairer base; the page selects it by default when the share is above 25%. Dividends are not part of any figure here: returns are price returns only.

  
Analyst figures versus your scenarios

  
Analyst price targets are typically 12-month views, and consensus EPS covers the current and next fiscal year. Your scenarios run to your holding period, so beyond the analyst horizon the numbers are assumptions, and a three-year result is mostly assumption. The result shows the year-by-year Base path so you can compare year 1 with analyst targets, and states how many years rest on your own assumptions.

  
Where the reference ranges come from

  
“Peers” are computed from data (your list, or Yahoo's largest companies in the industry). “Own history” is computed from the company's reported years. “Sector rule of thumb” is a hand-set long-run range, shown for context but never used in a calculation, not computed from data and not authoritative: it sits in core/multiples.py where you can edit it, and peers override it when available.

  
Data and limits

  
  • Data comes from Yahoo Finance through OpenBB and yfinance: provider fields, not filings. Check unusual figures against the company's report.
  • Analyst consensus is a mean with unverified GAAP/adjusted basis. Its relative periods (0y, +1y) are converted to dates from the provider's fiscal year end.
  • Growth is applied for exactly the holding period from the starting number; an older annual starting point therefore leaves out the growth since its year end, which the page warns about.
  • There is no walk-forward test of these defaults: the page does not give a hit rate or a confidence level, and the three methods are not independent evidence.
  • Growth is held flat unless you set Growth fades to; then it moves in a straight line from the starting rate to that rate by the exit date. Fast growers usually slow down, so a flat base case is optimistic for them.
  • No AI is used here: every number comes from code. Valuation snapshots are stored separately and never change your Plan.
